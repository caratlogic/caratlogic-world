## How to Keep Customer and Supplier Documents Current
Collecting a customer’s or supplier’s documents is only the beginning. Business details change, documents expire, contacts leave, and new records may replace older versions. If your team has no clear way to track these changes, it can be difficult to know whether a file is current or who should follow up.

A practical document management process helps your team see what was received, when it needs attention, and what action is still open. It also gives customers and suppliers a clearer way to provide updated information.

The process does not need to be complicated. Start by deciding which documents your business actually needs to maintain, record important dates and review status, assign responsibility for follow-up, and keep the latest approved version easy to find. The details should reflect your business, your relationships, and any requirements that apply to you.

There is no universal renewal schedule for every document or every diamond business. A printed expiry date, an internal review date, and a date when your company wants to re-confirm information are different things. Treat them separately and explain your process clearly.

## 1. Decide which documents need ongoing attention
Not every file needs an expiry reminder. Some documents have a clear expiration date. Others may remain valid until a business detail changes, or your company may choose to review them periodically.

Create a list of document types your business collects from customers and suppliers. Depending on your process, it may include business registration records, licences, proof of address, signed agreements, or other documents relevant to a particular relationship. Include a document only when it serves a real purpose for your business.

For each type, decide what should trigger a follow-up. That trigger might be a printed expiry date, a change in company details, a change in contact or ownership information, a new business relationship, or a scheduled review under your company’s own policy. Avoid describing an internal reminder date as a legal deadline unless it actually is one.

It can help to categorize records as:

  - **Date-based:** The document shows an expiry or renewal date.
  - **Change-based:** The document may need updating when a business detail changes.
  - **Policy-based:** Your company chooses to review it at a set interval.
  - **One-time:** The document is retained as a record and does not normally require renewal.

This simple distinction prevents unnecessary requests and helps employees understand why a particular document is being reviewed.

## 2. Build a document register
A document register is a central list showing what your business has received and what needs attention. It can be a spreadsheet or part of an onboarding or document management system.

For each record, consider tracking the business name, whether it is a customer or supplier, document type, submission date, printed expiry date if there is one, review status, person responsible, and next action. If a document has no expiry date, do not invent one. If your company has chosen a review date, label it as an internal review date.

The register should make it easy to answer practical questions: Which customers have documents approaching expiry? Which suppliers have not returned a requested update? Which file is the latest one? Who is responsible for the next follow-up?

Use consistent status labels, such as “Current,” “Review needed,” “Update requested,” “Updated file received,” or “Expired.” Define these labels so staff use them the same way. For example, “Updated file received” should not automatically mean “Reviewed and accepted.”

## 3. Record expiry dates accurately
When a document has an expiry date, record it from the document itself and check that the date is clear. Be careful with date formats. A date such as 04/05/2027 may be read differently in different countries. Use an unambiguous format in your internal record, such as “May 4, 2027,” or use a consistent year-month-day format.

If an expiry date is missing, illegible, or appears inconsistent, do not guess. Ask the customer or supplier for clarification or a clearer copy, following your business process.

Some files may not expire but may still become outdated. A company registration document, for example, might no longer reflect a current address or legal name after a change. Your team can record that such documents are reviewed when relevant business information changes, rather than assigning an artificial expiry date.

## 4. Set reminders with enough time for follow-up
A reminder should give your team time to contact the business, receive an updated file, and review it before the date becomes urgent. The right lead time depends on the document, how quickly the other party usually responds, and your internal process.

You might schedule an initial reminder ahead of an expiry date and a second follow-up if no response arrives. Make clear that these are your company’s follow-up intervals, not universal legal deadlines. Avoid sending repeated reminders if the document has already been replaced or the relationship no longer requires it.

Assign each reminder to a person or team. A notification without an owner can be overlooked. If responsibility changes, transfer the open reminder so the request does not disappear when an employee is away or changes roles.

## 5. Make update requests specific and easy to act on
When requesting a new document, tell the customer or supplier which file needs attention, why you are contacting them, how to submit the replacement, and when your team would like to receive it. If your business accepts alternatives, explain what they are.

For example, a useful message might say: “The business licence we have on file shows an expiry date of October 15. Please send us the renewed document through the secure upload form. Contact our team if the renewal is still being processed.”

Avoid vague messages such as “Please update your documents.” The recipient may not know which file you mean or what action is expected. A specific request reduces back-and-forth and makes it easier to complete the review.

If you are asking for updated information rather than a replacement document, say so directly. For instance, ask the person to confirm whether the registered address or main business contact has changed.

## 6. Review the replacement before marking it current
When an updated file arrives, record the date received and assign it for review. Check whether the document is readable, whether the relevant details match the business record, and whether it is suitable for the purpose for which your company requested it.

Keep the review outcome distinct from receipt. A file can be received but still be unclear, incomplete, or waiting for review. Use a status that reflects what has actually happened.

If the new document replaces an older version, mark the previous file as superseded or archived according to your recordkeeping process. Keep enough history for your team to understand what changed and when, while making the current version easy to identify. Avoid leaving several files labeled “final” without a clear indication of which is the latest.

## 7. Update related business information when it changes
Document renewal can reveal changes beyond the document itself. A new address, company name, licence detail, or business contact may require an update to the customer or supplier record.

Decide which fields should be checked when an updated document is reviewed. If a customer provides a new address, for example, your team may need to confirm whether it is the registered address, operating address, billing address, or another location. Do not overwrite one type of address with another without checking.

Also establish how customers and suppliers can tell your business about changes between scheduled document reviews. A clear contact route or update form can help keep records current without waiting for an expiry reminder.

## 8. Protect documents and limit access
Customer and supplier documents may contain sensitive business or personal information. Store them in a secure, organized location and limit access to employees who need the records for their work.

Decide how documents are shared internally, how changes are recorded, and how old or unnecessary files are handled. A retention policy should explain what your business needs to keep, for how long, how it is protected, and how it is securely disposed of when no longer needed. The Federal Trade Commission’s[business guide to protecting personal information](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/protecting-personal-information-guide-business) recommends that businesses collect only what they need, secure the information they keep, and establish appropriate retention and disposal practices.

Your document process should fit applicable privacy and recordkeeping obligations. If you are unsure what your business must retain or for how long, consult a qualified legal or compliance professional.

## A practical example
A US diamond dealer has a supplier’s business licence on file with a printed expiry date. The dealer records that date in its document register and assigns a team member to send a reminder before it expires. The supplier uploads a renewed file. The reviewer checks that it is readable and relates to the supplier, records the review outcome, and marks the previous version as superseded.

Separately, the supplier’s form indicates a new business address. The dealer checks which address changed, updates the correct field, and records when the update was made. This is an example of an internal document workflow, not a universal legal requirement or timeline.

## Frequently asked questions
### How often should customer and supplier documents be updated?
There is no single interval for every document or business. Follow any printed expiry date, update records when relevant details change, and use internal review intervals where your business has a reason to do so. Confirm legal or regulatory requirements with a qualified professional.

### Should every document have an expiry date in the system?
No. Record the expiry date shown on the document when one exists. For documents without an expiry date, track a change-based or internal review date only if your process calls for one. Do not create a date that could be mistaken for an official expiry.

### Does a reminder mean the document has been verified?
No. A reminder only prompts someone to follow up. When a replacement arrives, it still needs to be reviewed according to your business process. Keep the record’s status clear so employees can distinguish “received” from “reviewed.”

### What should happen to an old document after an update?
Follow your company’s retention and security policy. You may need to keep a record of the previous version, but label it clearly as superseded and make the current version easy to find. Retain and dispose of records according to applicable requirements and your business needs.

## Make document follow-up part of the workflow
Keeping customer and supplier documents current requires more than collecting files. Your business needs to know which records matter, what event triggers an update, who follows up, how replacements are reviewed, and where the current version is stored.

Start with a simple register and clear ownership. Use reminders for dates that matter, request updates in plain language, and distinguish a received document from one that has been reviewed. A consistent process helps your team maintain more reliable records while making document requests clearer for customers and suppliers.
