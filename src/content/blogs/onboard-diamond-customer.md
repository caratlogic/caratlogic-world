## How to Onboard a New Customer Before the First Diamond Trade
A new customer may be ready to discuss a stone, request a quotation, or arrange a first purchase. Before the trade moves forward, your business needs a clear way to understand who the customer is, record the details relevant to the relationship, and keep any supporting documents together.

That process is often called customer onboarding. In the diamond trade, it can include collecting business information, confirming contact details, recording the intended business relationship, reviewing documents, and deciding who in your company can approve the account. A consistent process helps your team handle new customers more clearly and makes it easier to find the information later.

Customer onboarding should also be easy for the customer to understand. If the first request is confusing, asks for information without explanation, or arrives through several separate emails, the customer may delay responding. A clear process shows what is needed, why it is being requested, and what happens after the form is submitted.

There is no single checklist that suits every diamond business. What you collect should reflect your business model, the type of customer, the markets involved, the value and nature of the transactions, and any rules that apply to your company. In the United States, AML requirements for dealers in precious stones, jewels, and related goods depend on the business and its activities. Check current requirements with a qualified legal or compliance professional; FinCEN provides information on the rules for covered businesses.

## 1. Decide what your onboarding process needs to cover
Before asking a customer for information, decide what your team needs to know and why. A wholesale dealer working with another established business may need different information from a retailer onboarding an individual buyer or a manufacturer opening a new trade account.

Start by defining the purpose of the onboarding process. For example, your business may need to identify the customer’s business, keep reliable contact details, understand the expected trading relationship, or maintain records for internal review. Each requested field should support a real business need.

This step prevents forms from becoming unnecessarily long. It also helps your team explain why information is being requested, which can make the process easier for the customer to complete. If a field does not help your team understand or manage the customer relationship, consider whether it needs to be included.

Decide who will review new submissions, who can approve an account, and who will contact the customer if something is missing. Even in a small business, assigning these responsibilities helps prevent forms from sitting unattended or being reviewed differently by different employees.

## 2. Collect the customer’s basic business details
For a business customer, a useful starting point is the company’s legal or registered name, trading name if different, business address, country of operation, website if available, and the name and role of the main contact. You may also record a business email address and phone number.

Ask for details in a consistent format. For example, separate the registered company name from the trading name instead of putting both into one open text field. This makes the information easier to review and search later. If the customer has multiple offices or contacts, specify which address or person should be used for account communications.

If the customer is an individual, collect only the information your process requires. Explain what is needed and how it will be used. Avoid gathering personal information simply because a form has room for it.

Use clear labels and examples where a field could be misunderstood. A customer should be able to tell whether you are asking for a registered business address, a shipping address, or the address of the person completing the form. Separating these details can reduce corrections later.

## 3. Understand the proposed trading relationship
Customer details alone may not tell your team what kind of account is being opened. Record the intended relationship in practical terms. Is the customer enquiring about a one-time purchase, ongoing wholesale supply, manufacturing work, or another type of trade?

Depending on your business, you may also want to record the product categories or services involved, the expected way orders will be placed, and the relevant business contact for quotations and invoices. The goal is to give staff enough context to handle the relationship consistently, rather than relying on scattered email conversations.

Keep this section factual. An onboarding form should not ask the customer to predict exact future order volumes if neither side knows them yet. Where estimates are useful, label them clearly as estimates. You can also ask whether the customer expects to purchase directly, through a purchasing contact, or through another agreed arrangement if that affects how your team handles orders.

Customer onboarding should remain distinct from recording the details of a specific stone or sale. Product descriptions, grading information, quotations, invoices, and shipment details belong in the relevant sales or inventory records. The customer profile should help your team understand the business relationship and find the right contact when a trade takes place.

## 4. Request supporting documents when they are relevant
Some businesses request documents to support details provided during onboarding. Depending on the customer and your process, these might include company registration information, a business licence, proof of address, or other records relevant to the relationship.

Tell customers which documents are required, which are optional, and what file formats you accept. If a document has an expiry date, record that date alongside the document. This gives your team a way to identify records that may need to be refreshed.

A document upload is not the same as independent verification. Your team should have a clear review step to check whether the document is readable, appears to match the information supplied, and is suitable for the purpose for which it was requested. If you use external verification or screening services, describe those separately and accurately.

Give customers a secure way to submit documents and explain what to do if they cannot provide a requested item. A short explanation or an alternative document may be appropriate under your own process. Record what was received and any follow-up that remains open so another employee can understand the account’s status.

## 5. Give customers a clear way to complete the form
A customer onboarding process should be straightforward to follow. Tell the customer who invited them, what information to prepare, how long the form is likely to take, and whom to contact if they have questions.

Use plain language in form labels. Instead of asking for “entity particulars,” ask for the company’s registered name. Instead of “beneficial ownership information” without explanation, provide a short description of what your business is asking for and why, where that information is relevant to your process.

For longer forms, group questions into sections such as business details, contacts, trading relationship, and documents. Let customers know which fields are required. If your process allows them to save and return later, explain how that works.

Before sending an invitation, check that it is going to the correct person and that the message explains the next step. If a customer receives an unexpected form without context, they may be unsure whether it is legitimate. Use a recognizable business email address and include a contact method they can use to confirm the request.

## 6. Review the submission before opening the account
When a customer submits the form, assign someone to review it. The reviewer can check whether required fields are complete, whether documents are attached where requested, and whether the information is consistent enough for your business to make a decision.

If something is missing or unclear, ask a specific follow-up question. “Please provide more information” can lead to extra back-and-forth. A clearer request would say which field or document needs attention and what the customer should provide.

Record the outcome of the review, who made the decision, and any follow-up that remains open. Your internal process might use outcomes such as approved, more information required, or declined. Choose labels that match how your team actually works and make their meaning clear to everyone involved.

If the account is approved, make sure the relevant employees know where to find the customer’s details and which contact should receive quotations, invoices, or order updates. If more information is required, keep the account in a status that makes the outstanding action visible. This avoids treating an incomplete submission as though it has been fully reviewed.

## 7. Keep customer records current
Onboarding is not always a one-time task. Contact details can change, companies can move, and documents may expire. Decide which customer details your business needs to keep current and how your team will follow up when information needs attention.

Where documents have expiry dates, record them in a place staff can monitor. Set a reminder before the expiry date so there is time to contact the customer and review an updated document. A reminder helps your team notice a date; it does not itself confirm that a renewed document is valid.

Also establish who can update a customer record and how changes should be recorded. This makes it easier to understand what information was received and when it changed. If an employee leaves or changes roles, make sure responsibility for customer follow-up is transferred to someone else.

## 8. Protect the information you collect
Customer onboarding can involve business and personal information. Limit access to people who need it for their work, use secure methods to collect and store documents, and set a retention approach that reflects applicable requirements and your business needs.

Tell customers how to contact your business with questions about the information they have submitted. Make sure staff know how to handle a request to correct a detail or replace a document. Clear ownership prevents customer information from being spread across inboxes, desktops, and separate spreadsheets.

A simple internal policy can explain where records are stored, which employees can access them, how corrections are handled, and when records should be reviewed or removed. Your approach should reflect your business’s legal obligations and privacy practices.

## A practical example
Consider a US diamond wholesaler opening an account for a new retailer. The wholesaler sends a form asking for the retailer’s business name, address, primary contact, and the type of trade relationship being requested. The retailer uploads the documents requested for that account. A staff member checks the submission, follows up on one unclear detail, and records the review outcome.

After approval, the wholesaler records which contact should receive quotations and invoices. If a document has an expiry date, the wholesaler records it and schedules a reminder to review it later. When the first order is placed, the stone and sale details are recorded in the appropriate business records, separate from the customer’s profile.

This is an illustrative workflow, not a universal legal checklist. Each business should adapt its process to its customers, transactions, internal controls, and applicable requirements.

## Common customer onboarding mistakes to avoid
One common mistake is asking every customer for the same extensive set of information, even when the relationship does not require it. Another is collecting documents without assigning anyone to review them. In both cases, the business may end up with more paperwork but little clarity about what has been checked or what remains incomplete.

Avoid sending forms without explaining why the customer is receiving them. Make sure your team uses consistent status labels, and avoid treating an email passcode or file upload as proof that someone’s identity or a document has been independently verified.

Finally, do not let the process end at submission. Define what happens next: who reviews the form, how missing information is requested, who records the outcome, and how the customer’s details are maintained over time.

## Frequently asked questions
### What information should a diamond business collect from a new customer?
The information depends on the business relationship and the company’s process. A business customer form may ask for company and contact details, the intended trading relationship, and supporting documents where relevant. Collect information for a clear purpose and check which requirements apply to your business.

### Should customer onboarding be completed before the first trade?
A business can set its own process for when onboarding must be completed. Many businesses choose to collect and review relevant information before opening an account or proceeding with a trade, so employees know how the customer relationship should be handled. Any timing policy should fit the business’s operations and applicable requirements.

### Is a customer onboarding form the same as identity verification?
No. A form collects information from a customer. Document review or identity verification may require separate steps, depending on your process and any applicable rules. Be precise when explaining what your business has reviewed or verified.

### How can a business follow up on documents that are expiring?
Record the document’s expiry date, decide how far in advance to send a reminder, and assign someone to review any replacement document. A reminder supports follow-up; the replacement still needs to be reviewed according to your business process.

## Prepare for the first trade with a repeatable process
A well-organized customer onboarding process gives your team a consistent way to collect relevant information, review submissions, follow up on missing details, and maintain records. It also tells customers what to expect before the first trade, which can make the beginning of a business relationship clearer for both sides.

Start with the information your business genuinely needs. Explain each request, assign responsibility for review, and decide how customer records will be kept current. Then refine the process as your customer base and operating needs change.
