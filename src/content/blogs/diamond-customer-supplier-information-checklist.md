## What Information Should You Collect from Diamond Customers and Suppliers?
When a diamond business begins working with a new customer or supplier, information often arrives through emails, forms, document attachments, and conversations with different employees. Without a consistent process, it can be hard to tell what has been received, what still needs review, and who is responsible for following up.

A clear onboarding process gives your team one place to collect and review relevant information about a new business relationship. It also helps customers and suppliers understand what your business is asking for and why.

The information you collect from a customer will not always be the same as the information you collect from a supplier. A customer record may focus on the buying relationship, account contacts, and invoicing details. A supplier record may need to cover the supplying business, product categories, business contacts, and other details relevant to sourcing. Some information may be useful for both.

There is no universal form or checklist that applies to every diamond business. Decide what to request based on your operations, the type of relationship, the information your business needs, and any requirements that apply to you. In the United States, rules for businesses dealing in precious stones, jewels, and related goods depend on whether the business and its activities fall within the relevant requirements. This article is an operational guide, not a legal checklist.

## Start with the purpose of each question
Before creating a customer or supplier form, write down why you need each piece of information. A field should help your team identify the business, communicate with the right person, manage the relationship, review a relevant document, or maintain a useful record.

This step keeps the form focused. If a question has no clear purpose, consider removing it. Asking for information that your team does not use can make onboarding feel intrusive and create records that are difficult to maintain.

It also helps to separate information needed before an account is opened from information needed later. For example, your team may need basic business and contact details at the start, while payment instructions or additional transaction information may be collected only when relevant to the relationship.

## Core information to consider for both customers and suppliers
For a business customer or supplier, your business may want to record:

  - Legal or registered business name and trading name, if different
  - Business address and country of operation
  - Main business email address and phone number
  - Name, role, and contact details of the person completing the form
  - Other relevant contacts, such as the person responsible for orders, accounts, or invoices
  - Type of business relationship being requested
  - Date the information was submitted and the status of its review

Use separate fields for details that may be different. For instance, a registered address, operating address, billing address, and shipping address serve different purposes. Ask only for the addresses your business actually needs.

Where a customer or supplier is an individual rather than a business, collect only the information relevant to that relationship and your process. Make clear which fields are required and explain how the information will be used.

## Information that may be useful for customer onboarding
A customer form should help your team understand who the customer is, how the account will be used, and which contacts should receive business communications. Depending on your business, consider collecting:

  - Customer type, such as retailer, wholesaler, manufacturer, or individual buyer
  - Intended relationship, such as a one-time purchase or ongoing trade account
  - Product categories or services the customer is interested in
  - Preferred contact for quotations, orders, and invoices
  - Billing and delivery details, where needed for the account
  - Any requested account or payment terms, if your business offers them

Keep expected purchase volumes or order frequency optional unless your business has a clear reason to request them. If you ask for estimates, label them as estimates. A new customer may not know how often they will order before a relationship has begun.

Record customer details separately from details about a specific diamond or transaction. A customer profile should identify and describe the business relationship. Product specifications, grading details, quotations, invoices, and shipping information should be recorded in the appropriate product or transaction records.

## Information that may be useful for supplier onboarding
A supplier form should help your team understand the supplying business and the practical details of the relationship. Depending on your sourcing process, you may want to record:

  - Supplier type, such as diamond dealer, gemstone supplier, manufacturer, or other trade partner
  - Product categories supplied
  - Main contact for availability, quotations, and orders
  - Contact responsible for invoices or account matters
  - Business locations relevant to the relationship
  - Payment or settlement details, when needed and handled through an appropriate secure process
  - Documents or declarations your business has decided to request from that supplier

Some businesses may also ask for information related to product sourcing, origin, or traceability where it is relevant to their purchasing process or internal policies. Do not present this as a universal requirement. Explain which information your business needs, and how it will be used.

Keep supplier payment details separate from general business profile information where possible. Access to sensitive payment data should be limited to employees who need it for their role. Establish a process for confirming changes to payment instructions before acting on them.

## Decide which supporting documents to request
Documents can support information provided by a customer or supplier, but a long document list does not automatically make a review more reliable. Choose documents based on the purpose of your process and the type of relationship.

Depending on the situation, a business may request company registration details, a business licence, proof of address, or another record relevant to the account. Some requests may apply only to certain customers, suppliers, locations, or types of trade. Clearly label documents as required or optional and tell the person what to do if they cannot provide a requested item.

For every document you collect, decide who will review it and what that review involves. A reviewer might check whether the file is readable, whether the name or address appears consistent with the submitted form, and whether the document is suitable for the stated purpose. Uploading a file is not the same as independently verifying it.

If the document has an expiry date, record that date with the document. Set a reminder if your process calls for one, and assign someone to review any replacement document. A reminder can prompt follow-up, but it does not confirm that a document is valid.

## Explain the request in plain language
A form is easier to complete when people understand what it is asking. Use simple field labels such as “Registered business name” or “Main contact for orders.” Avoid unexplained technical terms or abbreviations.

If you request a document or detail that may be unfamiliar, add a short explanation beside the field. Tell the customer or supplier which file formats you accept, how to submit the information, and whom to contact if they have a question. If the form takes several minutes to complete, say so in the invitation.

For longer forms, group information into sections such as business details, contacts, trading relationship, and documents. Make required fields clear. If the form allows someone to save their progress and return later, explain how to do that before they start.

## Create a simple review and follow-up process
After a customer or supplier submits a form, someone should review it. The review may include checking that required fields are complete, that requested documents have been attached, and that information is clear enough for your team to proceed.

If something is missing, send a specific request. Instead of writing “Please provide more information,” identify the field or document and say what needs to be added or clarified. Record the request and any response so another employee can see the status.

Use review outcomes that reflect your actual workflow, such as “Under review,” “More information required,” “Approved,” or “Declined.” Define what each status means internally. Record who reviewed the submission and when, along with any follow-up that remains open.

Approval should have a clear meaning in your business. For example, it may mean the onboarding form has been reviewed and the account can move to the next step in your process. Avoid implying that approval confirms every fact, document, or future transaction unless your review process actually establishes that.

## Keep records current and protect the information
Contact details, addresses, business roles, and documents can change. Decide which information your business needs to keep current and how staff should record updates. For documents with expiry dates, choose when reminders should be sent and who is responsible for reviewing replacements.

Protect the information you collect. Limit access to employees who need it, use secure methods to collect and store documents, and establish how corrections or replacement files are handled. Your business should also decide how long records are kept in line with its needs and applicable requirements.

## A practical example
A US diamond wholesaler is considering a new retailer as a customer and a new dealer as a supplier. For the retailer, the wholesaler collects the business name, address, primary contact, intended account type, and the right contact for quotations and invoices. For the dealer, it collects the supplier’s business and contact details, product categories, and any documents the wholesaler has decided are relevant to its sourcing process.

A staff member reviews each submission, follows up on unclear details, and records the review status. The wholesaler keeps payment information in a restricted area and records expiry dates for documents that need later follow-up. This is an illustrative workflow, not a universal legal checklist; each business should set its own process according to its operations and applicable requirements.

## Frequently asked questions
### What information should I collect from a new diamond customer?
Start with the details your business needs to identify the customer, communicate with the right people, and manage the proposed relationship. This may include business and contact details, the type of account, and selected documents where relevant. The exact fields depend on your process and applicable requirements.

### What information should I collect from a diamond supplier?
A supplier onboarding form may include business and contact details, product categories, relevant locations, and documents your business has chosen to request. Some businesses also collect sourcing or traceability information when it is relevant to their purchasing process or internal policies.

### Should I use the same form for customers and suppliers?
Some details may be common to both, but the business relationship is different. A shared form can collect core business and contact details, while separate sections or forms cover customer-specific and supplier-specific information.

### Does collecting a document mean it has been verified?
No. Collecting a document means your business has received a file. Verification requires a separate review or process. Describe accurately what your team checks and what it does not check.

## Build a checklist that fits your business
A useful diamond customer and supplier onboarding checklist is clear, focused, and connected to a real process. Decide what information you need from each type of business relationship, explain why you are asking for it, assign someone to review submissions, and keep records in a place your team can manage.

Review the form periodically. If employees repeatedly have to ask the same follow-up questions, clarify the relevant field. If a question is no longer useful, remove it. A well-maintained process helps your business collect the right information without making onboarding harder than it needs to be.
