## Why have a supplier onboarding process?
A new supplier offers a selection of diamonds that could fit your customers’ needs. Before your business begins regular orders, you need to know who you are dealing with, what information to request, and who on your team will review it.

A **supplier onboarding process** gives your business a consistent way to handle those first steps. It can help keep business details, supporting documents, review notes, and follow-ups together. It also makes it easier for staff to see what has been received and what still needs attention.

The process should fit your business and the supplier relationship. A supplier offering polished stones may require different information from one supplying rough material or providing manufacturing services. Your internal policy and any applicable rules also matter. A checklist or software workflow can help organize the work, but it does not decide what your business must verify.

## Step 1: Decide what information you need
Before sending a form, decide what your business needs to know about each new supplier. Start with a standard set of business details, then add questions that apply to the supplier’s role, products, location, and relationship with your company.

Your basic information may include the supplier’s legal business name, business address, country of registration, main contact details, and the name and role of the person completing the form. Depending on your process, you may also request registration information, relevant licenses or certificates, payment details, and information about ownership or authorized representatives.

For a supplier involved in diamond sourcing, you may need questions about the supplier’s products, sourcing practices, or traceability information. These are examples to consider, not a universal checklist. Ask for documents that support your process, and explain why each request is relevant.

This first step helps keep supplier onboarding practical. If you send every supplier the same long form regardless of what they provide, you may create unnecessary work for both sides. A tailored form can still use consistent core questions while adding relevant sections for different supplier types.

## Step 2: Send a clear invitation
Tell the supplier what the form is for, what information to prepare, and how to submit it. If you require supporting documents, name them clearly. A request such as “upload your current business registration document” is easier to act on than “send compliance paperwork.”

Set a contact person for questions. Suppliers may be working across time zones, using a phone rather than a computer, or unfamiliar with your process. Clear instructions and a straightforward form can reduce confusion and incomplete submissions.

For a U.S. business dealing with an overseas supplier, avoid assuming that a supplier’s country alone determines the level of review needed. FinCEN guidance for certain covered dealers discusses risk assessment related to foreign suppliers, while recognizing that risk varies by supplier and business circumstances. Whether a specific business is covered depends on the relevant rules and facts.[ ](https://www.fincen.gov/resources/statutes-regulations/guidance/guidance-dealers-including-certain-retailers-precious)

## Step 3: Collect business details and supporting documents
Once the supplier responds, collect the information through the channel your business has chosen and check that the response is complete. Depending on your process, the supplier may provide company registration details, relevant trade licenses, certificates, ownership information, financial or payment information, and sourcing or supply chain details.

Keep the supplier’s answers with the documents they relate to. This makes it easier for the reviewer to compare the information and follow up on gaps. If a document is unreadable, incomplete, or not the one requested, explain the issue and say what the supplier should send instead.

Be thoughtful about sensitive data. Limit requests to information relevant to your process, control who can access submitted documents, and follow your company’s policies for storing and retaining them.

A published example from the jewelry industry is Brilliant Earth’s supplier onboarding requirements. Its public process describes collecting information about supplier operations and sourcing, along with supply chain and compliance documentation. This illustrates how one company has made its requirements visible to suppliers; it does not mean every diamond business needs the same questions or documents.

## Step 4: Review the submission and follow up
Assign someone to review the supplier’s answers and documents. The reviewer should know what to check, how to record the review, and when to ask for clarification. Make a note of missing fields, conflicting details, or documents that need further attention.

If information is incomplete, send a specific request. For example: “The form lists the registered company name as ABC Trading LLC, but the uploaded registration document shows ABC Gems LLC. Please confirm which entity will be supplying the goods and upload the relevant document.” A clear question helps the supplier respond without guessing what the reviewer needs.

Keep a record of what was reviewed and any decision made under your company’s process. An internal status such as “awaiting documents,” “under review,” or “review complete” can help staff understand where the file stands. These labels describe your workflow; they do not by themselves confirm legal compliance or validate the supplier’s claims.

## Step 5: Make and record an internal decision
After review, follow the approval process your business has established. A supplier may be ready to work with your team, may need to provide more information, or may require additional review before a decision is made.

Record who made the decision, when it was made, and any conditions or follow-up tasks. If the supplier is not approved, follow your business’s procedure for communicating that decision and retaining the record.

For businesses that are subject to applicable anti-money laundering requirements, onboarding should fit within the wider policies and controls set for the business. Software can help organize forms and review stages, but it cannot replace a company’s judgment or professional advice. FinCEN’s guidance concerns covered dealers and certain retailers; it should not be read as saying that every U.S. jewelry business has the same obligations.[ ](https://www.fincen.gov/resources/statutes-regulations/guidance/guidance-dealers-including-certain-retailers-precious)

## Step 6: Keep the supplier record current
Supplier onboarding continues after the first review. Business contacts change, documents expire, and a supplier’s role or product range may change. Your process should say which records need updating, who follows up, and when reviews should happen.

Use reminders for documents with expiry dates, and record when the supplier provides an updated copy. If the nature of the relationship changes, review whether the information on file is still suitable for your process. Avoid treating a completed onboarding form as a permanent confirmation that every detail will remain current.

The Responsible Jewellery Council’s Code of Practices includes a Know Your Counterparty provision for relevant suppliers and customers of materials within the standard’s scope. It also calls for enhanced KYC in defined consignment situations. The standard has a specific membership and material scope; it is not a universal checklist for every supplier relationship.[ ](https://www.responsiblejewellery.com/wp-content/uploads/RJC-2024-COP-Standard.pdf)

## Supplier onboarding checklist
Before marking a supplier file complete, check that your team has:

  - Collected the business information required by your process.
  - Received the documents relevant to that supplier relationship.
  - Reviewed the answers and noted any follow-up.
  - Recorded who reviewed the file and the outcome.
  - Assigned responsibility for future updates and document renewals.

The information requested will depend on your business, the supplier, and the rules or standards that apply. Have your compliance or legal advisor review your process where needed.
